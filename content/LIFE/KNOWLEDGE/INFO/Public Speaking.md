# Public Speaking

## 🗒️ Description

Hub for everything in this vault about giving talks: the researched playbook, the canonical books, and
the toolchain. Three sources approach the same craft from different directions and disagree in useful
places, so this note holds the map rather than the material.

I speak occasionally as a practitioner — conference talks of 25–45 minutes, community meetups,
webinars, client sessions and cohort workshops, delivered in Polish to mostly technical audiences.
The material here is cut for that, not for professional speaking.

## 🧩 Where the three sources agree

- **Delivery is trained, not innate.** Carnegie's swimming metaphor and Anderson's example of a shy
  12-year-old coached into a strong TED talk in six months make the same claim a century apart.
- **The fear is real and is not the obstacle.** Both treat stage fright as something to master rather
  than eliminate. Gladstone and Beecher never lost it.
- **Monotony kills a talk faster than weak content.** Carnegie's one-keyed piano; Anderson's closing
  advice to vary the voice.
- **One idea per visual, and only where the visual reveals something words cannot.** Anderson states
  it directly; the research adds the cognitive-load evidence behind it.

## ⚔️ Where they contradict each other

**Script or improvise.** Carnegie says memorise at least the opening sentences. Anderson says a
memorised talk *sounds* memorised, and reports that TED dropped its own no-scripts rule when Daniel
Kahneman could not work under it. [[Conference Talk Craft]] resolves this the way I actually work:
verbatim on the seams, free in the middle — which removes the single-point-of-failure risk on both
sides.

**Feeling versus structure.** Carnegie's load-bearing claim is that gesture and persuasion must
emanate from genuine feeling, and that technique without it looks like branches nailed to a tree.
The research is largely structural — checkpoints, cut lists, protocols. These are complementary, but
the structural material can quietly crowd out the thing Carnegie says actually moves a room.

**Arranging the audience.** Carnegie recommends seating people densely so reaction spreads by
contagion. At a conference the seating is not mine to arrange, and the framing is closer to crowd
manipulation than I want to work in. Noted, not adopted.

## ⚠️ Read the evidence grading before quoting any of this

Roughly a third of the standard advice in this field is a sound practice attached to a debunked
justification — the ten-minute attention rule, Mehrabian's 7-38-55 ratio, power posing, the recency
effect as a reason to close after Q&A. Both books above repeat at least one of these in passing.
[[Conference Talk Craft]] carries the full list with sources, and the practices mostly survive once
the wrong reason is removed.
