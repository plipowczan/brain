# Delivery and Stage Operations

## 🗒️ Description

Everything that happens between arriving at the venue and leaving it, researched as the second wave
behind [[Conference Talk Craft]]. Eleven techniques: how I sound and stand, what to do with nerves
and with a failure, how a recorded talk differs from a room talk, and the operational items — stage
tech, the question channel, the contract with the host, a resettable demo, a room check and a backup
kit. The operational half is mostly practitioner consensus built from failure reports; the delivery
half has more research behind it and, as usual, more folklore on top.

Siblings: [[Talk Narrative and Structure]], [[Slides for Technical Talks]],
[[Practising and Reviewing Talks]].

## 🧩 The techniques

### Voice, body and nerves

| Technique | What it is | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Four separately trainable controls. Pace: measured in syllables per second, not a borrowed words-per-minute figure. Pauses: 15–20 planned silent ones of 1–3 seconds around the key sentences. Projection: sized to the microphone, not the room. Fillers: cut down, not out, by habit reversal. Measure each from a recording. | Pauses high and nearly free; fillers medium |
| **Body, stage position, pointer** | Stand beside the screen where I can see both the slide and the room (Weatherman), not behind the lectern (Bunker). Let hands gesture. Point with the body or with a highlight built into the slide, and treat the laser as an occasional tool (Lightsaber), not a crutch (Laser Weapons). | Medium; slide-built highlights high for recorded talks |
| **Speaking anxiety** | In the last minutes, relabel the racing heart as readiness ("I am excited") instead of trying to calm down, remind myself the audience sees far less than I feel, use slow long exhales if I want a physical lever, skip power posing. The one intervention with a large effect is repeated exposure. | Medium; cheap enough to always do |

### On camera and when something breaks

| Technique | What it is | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Camera versus room** | Give a recorded conference talk to the room, not to the lens — serving a live and a remote audience at once is the Dual-Headed Monster. Then patch the six things the recording silently loses with cheap verbal habits: unrepeated questions, show-of-hands results, laser and hand pointing, room laughter, references to the day, and me outside the frame. Anything aimed mainly at online viewers is a separate recording. | High for recorded and reused talks |
| **Recovery protocol** | Written in advance: name the problem in one plain sentence, act (fix inside a 30–60 second time-box or switch straight to the fallback), bridge back with a prepared line, and never mention it again. | High |
| **Stage tech** | Settle five things before the day: which microphone (a headset or lavalier frees both hands), which clicker and which keys it sends, whether a confidence monitor exists and what it shows, the display's native resolution and aspect ratio, and where the presenter view will physically live — my laptop in Extend mode, a monitor at the stage edge, or nowhere, in which case the notes go on a card. | High |

### Question handling infrastructure

| Technique | What it is | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Anonymous question channel** | Let the room type questions on their phones during the talk, anonymously with upvotes (Slido, Mentimeter or the event app). A moderator reads the top ones at Q&A. The queue exists before the floor opens, nobody has to stand up, and I leave with a written list of every question. Needs the organiser's agreement. | High for large, late or recorded talks where the organiser already runs a tool; otherwise medium |
| **Contract with the moderator** | One card agreed with the host in two minutes: whose clock counts and when my time ends by the wall clock; signals at 5, 2 and 0 minutes and where the host sits so I can see them; who picks questions and by what rule; who cuts a monologue; and 30 seconds for my close after the last answer. | High: the cheapest structural fix for timing and Q&A |

### Demo and kit

| Technique | What it is | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **One-command demo reset** | Every demo state reachable with one short command: a reset script that returns the repo, data and terminal to the starting state and checks that it did, a tagged checkpoint per demo stage, and long commands and prompts prepared in advance so I never type them live. | High for a live or hybrid demo that changes state |
| **Room recon and tech check** | Get into the actual room before the audience does, or into the changeover minutes, and run one fixed script: deck and video on the real screen at its real resolution, the microphone at stage volume, my smallest text read from the back row, where I stand and where my eyes go for notes and the clock, and whether the stage lights let me see the room at all. | High |
| **Backup equipment checklist** | A written list ranked by how badly each failure hurts: my own video cable and an adapter for the port the laptop really has, the charger, a clicker I know, and the deck in at least three independent places off the laptop — two USB sticks carried separately, a cloud link that needs no login, and a copy already with the organiser. Every copy holds the whole build. | High |

## ⚖️ What the evidence actually supports

### Sound practice, wrong justification

| Practice | The usual reason | What the research says |
|---|---|---|
| Cut filler words | "Fillers make people stop understanding you" and "they are just nerves" | They signal planning difficulty, and a filled pause is rated better than an equally long dead silence (Christenfeld 1995). The goal is fewer, not zero, and the replacement is a short intentional pause, not faster speech. Words after a silent pause are recognised better (MacGregor et al. 2010). The claim that fillers help recall is contested after a partial replication failed. |
| Speak at 130–150 wpm | An English coaching convention | No transfer to Polish, and words per minute mixes speaking time with pauses. Fluent delivery raises ratings, not learning (Carpenter et al. 2013). |
| Body language | "55% of the message" (Mehrabian 7-38-55) | That study concerned contradictory emotional signals in single words, not informational talks. The practices — open body, free hands, deliberate position — are sound for other reasons: gesturing lightens the speaker's working-memory load (Goldin-Meadow 2001) and an instructor pointing improves learning from video (Pi 2017). |
| Power pose backstage | "It makes you confident" | Debunked as a performance or hormone intervention; later studies find only self-reported effects. |
| Reappraise arousal | "Say 'I am excited' and you will perform better" | Brooks 2014 is real, but a 2025 direct replication in public speaking reproduced only the self-reported effects and observers rated the speeches no differently. Across reappraisal trials the performance effect is small (d = 0.23, Bosshard & Gomez 2024). Worth doing as a cheap mood intervention, not as a performance guarantee. Repeated exposure is the intervention with the large effect (Ebrahimi 2019, g = 0.74, rising to 1.11 at follow-up); virtual-reality exposure roughly matches real exposure. |
| Brief acknowledgement of a mishap | "A mistake makes you more likeable" | The pratfall effect holds only for people already seen as competent. What is solid: observers judge blunders less harshly than speakers expect (Savitsky, Epley & Gilovich 2001), and only a repeated apology turns a glitch into the story of the talk. |
| Anonymous question tool | "Upvotes surface the best questions" and "it fixes the gender gap" | One early upvote raised final ratings by 25% in a randomised study (Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013), so vote ranking snowballs; the moderator should not treat the top as fixed. Questions typed into a named chat showed the same gender gap as spoken ones (Zhang et al. 2023), so default anonymity is what matters. The classic disaster was a named Twitter feed projected behind the speaker without moderation: the failure was the projection, not anonymity. |
| Time signals | "A time card keeps a speaker on time" | Enforcement does. How strictly limits were enforced was among the main predictors of overrunning across 11 conferences (Edlund et al. 2018). |
| Demo reset | "It makes the demo deterministic" | False for demos built on LLM calls: a reset restores an agent's inputs, not its outputs. |

### Where the evidence is thin

- **The anonymous channel** has field reports from classrooms and one radiology conference (324
  anonymous texted questions against 42 paper notes), small studies and vendor data, but no controlled
  comparison with microphone Q&A at a conference. Free tiers limit participants and moderation, and the
  limits change — check the plan before relying on one.
- **The contract with the moderator** rests on chair instructions from many conferences plus partial
  field evidence. One conference that offered the first question after every talk to a trainee saw
  women's share of questions rise and stay higher (Telis et al. 2019). An astronomy-meeting result
  linking the chair's gender to who asked did not replicate clearly elsewhere (Davenport 2014).
- **Operations items** — recon, backup kit, stage tech, demo reset — are practitioner consensus.
  The numbers attached (arrival times, minimum font sizes) are conventions, not findings. The back-row
  geometry is the exception: code and terminals need the farthest viewer within about four times the
  image height, large slide text about six times, from an AV standard.
- **A demo reset** has one contested point: one practitioner found a commit per demo step unworkable
  because of constant rebasing and moved to numbered step directories. Also, `git clean -fdx` deletes
  caches I pre-downloaded on purpose.

### Small facts that bite

- Presenter view needs **Extend** mode; venues default to Duplicate. HDMI audio routing is a separate
  setting. Narrating a silent demo clip removes one thing that can fail.
- A laser dot is invisible on LCD/LED walls and in slide-feed recordings. Build highlights into separate
  slides.
- A USB-C port carries video only with DisplayPort Alt Mode or Thunderbolt.
- On some clickers the start button sends F5, which reloads a browser deck and drops full screen; the
  Marp HTML deck has no handler for the blank-screen key.
- An HTML deck copied without its media folder plays black rectangles. A copy only counts if it is
  independent *and* complete.
- Standard Whisper drops filler words, so automatic filler counts look better than the truth.
- Advice about confidence monitors conflicts: one source says cover it, another says put one at the
  speaker's feet. Decide per room.

## 🔧 Cheapest first moves

- **Failure card**: write the name-it line, the time-box and the bridge line for the three failures I
  fear most, then have someone take the clicker away mid-run and see whether the card is enough.
- **Host card**: the seven lines above on a single A5 card, handed over in person.
- **Adapter drill**: from the laptop in its bag to the first slide full screen on an external display with
  presenter view on the laptop, timed.
- **Pause pass**: mark 15–20 planned pauses in the presenter notes for one block and rehearse only that block.
