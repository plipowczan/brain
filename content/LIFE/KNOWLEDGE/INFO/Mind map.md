# Mind map

## 🗒️ Description
It is a note taking method for better brain engagement. When making a mind map, we engage not only the left, but also the right hemisphere of the brain, use spatial memory, build a system of associations, making it easier and faster to remember information and later reproduce it from memory.  

Creating a mind map (mind map) is to jot down a certain topic using arrows or lines connected to each other and the resulting keywords that we extract from a text or lecture. A note made in this way has a radial structure that maps a sequence of associations starting from a central keyword.

> [!warning] Evidence check (2026-11-13)
> The "left and right hemisphere" part of the description above is a popular myth, not science. A 2013 study scanned the brains of 1,011 people aged 7–29. Some functions do sit more on one side, region by region, but no one was "left-brained" or "right-brained" overall (Nielsen et al., *PLOS ONE*). Mind maps can still help, but for other reasons: you have to pick keywords (selection), arrange them by importance (organisation) and connect them to what you already know (elaboration). Those three are what the learning research rewards. See "What the evidence says" below.

### Where the idea comes from
Tony Buzan popularised the mind map in the 1970s, mainly through his BBC series and book *Use Your Head* (1974). Branching diagrams of ideas existed long before him. Buzan gave them a name, a set of rules (one central image, one keyword per branch, colour, curved branches) and a brand.

### Mind map vs concept map
The two get confused, but they are different tools:

| | Mind map | Concept map |
|---|---|---|
| Centre | One central topic | No single centre — a network |
| Links | Unlabelled branches (parent → child) | Labelled links ("causes", "is part of", "contradicts") |
| Shape | Tree, radial | Graph, cross-links allowed |
| Best for | Overview, brainstorming, planning, recall drills | Explaining how ideas relate, causal chains |

A [[Zettelkasten]] is closer to a concept map: notes link to each other in a network, not to one centre. A mind map is a good *entry point* into such a network: one map per topic, with branches pointing to the notes.

## 🧩Features:

Mind maps are visually appealing, catch the eye, and are easy to remember, so you can use them during:
- learning,
- reading (books, articles), but also writing texts,
- planning and setting strategies and goals (anything),
- noting the development of your ideas/projects,
- presenting your ideas, an issue,
- brainstorming (both individual and group),
- taking notes during a staff meeting, training.

### Example: a book chapter as a mind map
A mind map does not need to be drawn to be useful. This is the same structure written as an indented list, which is how most mind-map plugins store it anyway:

```text
How to Take Smart Notes
├── Three note types
│   ├── Fleeting — capture, throw away after processing
│   ├── Literature — short, own words, with source
│   └── Permanent — one idea, written for future me
├── Slip-box
│   ├── Link to existing notes
│   └── Index = entry points by keyword
└── Why it works
    ├── Writing externalises thinking
    └── Elaboration > collecting
```

Five minutes of this after reading a chapter gives you the "map" of the chapter. The permanent notes then hold the detail.

### What the evidence says
- **Maps beat passive study.** A meta-analysis of 55 studies with 5,818 participants (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006, *Review of Educational Research*) linked learning with concept and knowledge maps to better retention than reading texts, attending lectures or discussions. The advantage was small when maps were compared with other structured formats such as outlines and lists.
- **Making a map helps more than studying one.** The 2018 update of that meta-analysis (Schroeder et al., *Educational Psychology Review*, 142 effect sizes) found an overall effect of g = 0.58: 0.72 for students who *built* maps, 0.43 for students who studied ready-made ones.
- **Mind maps specifically:** a meta-analysis of 21 studies (Shi et al., *Asia Pacific Education Review*, 2022) found that mind mapping beat traditional instruction on learning outcomes, with the size of the effect depending on subject and grade.
- **The classic medical-school study is weaker than its reputation.** Farrand, Hussain & Hennessy (2002, *Medical Education*) tested 50 medical students. A week later the mind-map group recalled about 10% more of a text than the control group, but that difference was not statistically clear on its own. It reached about 15% once the mind-map group's lower motivation was taken into account. So it helped recall, and the students didn't enjoy it.
- **The gain comes from the processing, not from colour or shape.** Making a map forces selection and organisation. Copying someone else's map, or decorating your own, adds little.
- **Weak spots:** maps are poor at holding argument, nuance and sources, and they are hard to search. For anything you want to keep for years, the map should point to proper notes rather than replace them.

### When to use a mind map — and when not
- ✅ First pass over a new topic or book: what are the big parts?
- ✅ Planning a talk, a project, an article: what goes where?
- ✅ Recall drill before an exam or a talk: redraw the map from memory, then compare with the original.
- ❌ Keeping detailed knowledge long-term: that goes into linked notes ([[Zettelkasten]]).
- ❌ Memorising facts for good: flashcards with spaced reviews do that better ([[Spaced Repetition and Flashcards from Your Own Notes]]).
