# Pomodoro

## 🗒️ Description
Is a technique of working on the tasks.

More precisely: a way to work in short, fixed, uninterrupted blocks of time with planned breaks between them. Francesco Cirillo came up with it in the late 1980s, in his first years at university, when he bet himself he could study for just a few minutes without drifting off, timed with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. *Pomodoro* is Italian for tomato. By his own account the technique was defined in 1992. He self-published it as a free PDF in 2006 and as a book in 2018.

The method is not just "25 minutes on, 5 off". Cirillo describes five stages:
1. **Planning** — at the start of the day, choose from the *Activity Inventory* (your full task list) what goes on the *To Do Today* sheet.
2. **Tracking** — during the day, mark every finished pomodoro and every interruption next to the task.
3. **Recording** — at the end of the day, copy the numbers into a *Records* sheet.
4. **Processing** — turn the records into information: how many pomodoros did that kind of task really take?
5. **Visualizing** — look at the trends and adjust how you estimate and plan.

Three sheets of paper (To Do Today, Activity Inventory, Records) and a timer are the whole toolkit.

## 🧩Features:
Helps you if:
- you want to stay focus on the task
- can't beat the procrastination
- find little distractions often derail the whole workday
- consistently work past the point of optimal productivity
- have lots of open-ended work that could take unlimited amounts of time (e.g., studying for an exam, research for a blog post, etc.)
- re overly optimistic when it comes to how much you can get done in a day (aren't we all 🙃)  
- enjoy gamified goal-setting

How to:
1. Pick a task
2. Set a 25-minute timer
3. Work on the task until the time is up
4. Take a 5 minute break
5. Every 4 pomodoros, take a longer 15-30 minute break

### The rules that make it work
- **A pomodoro is indivisible.** If it gets interrupted for real, it doesn't count. Start a new one.
- **If a pomodoro begins, it has to ring.** Finished early? Use the remaining minutes to review what you just did ("overlearning").
- **Internal interruptions** (the urge to check email, a sudden idea): mark an apostrophe `'` next to the task, write the urge down under *Unplanned & Urgent*, and keep going until the timer rings.
- **External interruptions** (other people): mark a dash `-` and use **inform, negotiate, call back**. Tell them you're in the middle of something, agree on a time, and get back to them as promised.
- **The break is a real break.** Cirillo's break is 3–5 minutes. No phone, no inbox. Walk, stretch, get water. [[Learn Like a Pro]] adds the reason: the brain consolidates during the break.
- **Tasks bigger than 5–7 pomodoros get split.** Tasks smaller than one get grouped with others.
- **25 minutes is a default, not a law.** Cirillo's point is a fixed block you don't negotiate with while it runs. Some people prefer longer blocks, such as 50/10, for deep work. Whatever length you pick, keep it fixed.

### Example day
| Task | Estimate | Done | Interruptions |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Write chapter outline | 2 | 🍅🍅 | `'` (urge to check mail, written down) |
| Review flashcards | 1 | 🍅 | — |
| Fix the login bug | 3 | 🍅🍅🍅🍅 | `-` (colleague, called back at 14:00) |

At the end of the day: the bug took 4 instead of 3. Next estimate for that kind of work goes up.

### What the evidence says
The research is small and mixed. There is no proper meta-analysis yet, and most claims online are anecdotal.
- **Biwer et al. (2023)**, 87 university students in real self-study. Students who took breaks "when they felt like it" studied and rested longer. They also reported more fatigue and distraction and less concentration and motivation than students on fixed Pomodoro-style breaks (24/6 or 12/3). Task completion was the same, so fixed breaks got the same work done in less time.
- **Smits, Wenzel & de Bruin (2025)**, a randomised trial with 94 students. Fatigue rose faster during Pomodoro (25/5) sessions, but overall fatigue did not differ from self-regulated breaks. The expected advantage of fixed breaks did not show up.
- **Ogut (2025)**, a scoping review of 32 studies. It reports roughly 20% lower fatigue with structured breaks than with self-paced ones, but rates the certainty as moderate to low.
- Bottom line: fixed breaks probably help some people manage fatigue and get started. They are not a proven performance boost. The underlying ideas (single-tasking, regular short breaks) are on firmer ground than the 25-minute number.

### My experience
From my notes on [[Learn Like a Pro]] (2025): the technique doesn't work for me. I lack the motivation to start the timer. I struggle to stay on one task, and I skip the breaks because stopping in the middle of something isn't intuitive for me. I know it helps with focus. I just don't follow the rules, so the problem is me, not the method. What does work for me is self-explanation in my own words and leaving hard problems for the diffuse mode.

If you are like me, try the smallest version: one pomodoro, on one task, with the phone in another room. Count it as a win even if the rest of the day is chaos.
