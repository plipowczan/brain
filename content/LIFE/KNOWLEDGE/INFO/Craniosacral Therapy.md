# Craniosacral Therapy

> [!info] Context
> Parked research item (PL-247): terapia czaszkowo-krzyżowa, which is widely offered in Poland by physiotherapists and osteopaths. This note keeps **what CST claims** strictly apart from **what systematic reviews found**. It's built from PubMed abstracts and full-text reviews; the sources are at the bottom.

**Evidence key:** 🟢 strong (consistent systematic reviews) · 🟡 moderate / conflicting · 🔴 weak / untested.

## 🗒️ The honest verdict

Craniosacral therapy (CST) is **gentle, usually pleasant, and low-risk** when done as true light touch. Two things don't hold up:
- **The mechanism is not supported.** Therapists cannot agree on the rhythm they say they feel, and 5 g of touch has not been shown to move skull bones.
- **There is no clinically meaningful benefit.** Three independent systematic reviews from 2024 find none for any condition studied, on very-low-certainty evidence.

The one favourable meta-analysis rests on 2–4 sham-controlled trials, one of them the authors' own. Any relief people feel is best explained by **non-specific effects**: touch, relaxation, attention, expectation, placebo. If I go, I go for relaxation, not to treat a condition.

## 🧩 What CST claims

- **Method:**
  - Very light touch, around **5 g** (the "weight of a nickel"), on the skull, spine and sacrum.
  - The therapist feels for a "rhythmic, tide-like motion" and works to "release restrictions" in the membranes and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) around the brain and spinal cord.
- **The model**, Sutherland's **"primary respiratory mechanism"**, has five parts:
  1. the brain and spinal cord move on their own;
  2. the CSF fluctuates;
  3. the membranes inside the skull and spine are under reciprocal tension;
  4. the skull bones move at their sutures;
  5. the sacrum moves involuntarily, in step with the skull.
- **The rhythm**, the "cranial rhythmic impulse":
  - usually quoted as **6–12 cycles/min**;
  - textbooks give anything from 6 to 15;
  - measured palpated rates scatter widely, e.g. ~4.5 ± 2 per minute.
- **SomatoEmotional Release:** Upledger's extension, which claims the body stores emotional energy from trauma and that treatment can release it.
- **Marketed uses:** the Upledger Institute lists 23 conditions without citing studies:
  - migraine and headache, chronic neck and back pain, TMJ;
  - concussion and traumatic brain injury (TBI);
  - ADHD and autism, "pediatric challenges", infant colic;
  - PTSD and stress;
  - Alzheimer's, immune disorders, scoliosis, cancer support.

## 🧩 Origins

- **William Garner Sutherland (1873–1954):**
  - An osteopath who began studying the skull as a student around 1899 and published *The Cranial Bowl* in 1939.
  - His model became **"osteopathy in the cranial field"** (cranial osteopathy), practised within osteopathy.
  - After 1945 he moved to a no-force "Breath of Life" approach.
- **John Upledger:**
  - In 1970, during surgery, he saw what he described as rhythmic movement of the spinal dura.
  - From 1975 to 1983 he did clinical research at Michigan State University.
  - He coined **CranioSacral Therapy**, published the textbook in 1983 and founded the **Upledger Institute** in 1985.
  - CST is the offshoot taught widely to non-osteopaths: physiotherapists, massage therapists and others. Research usually lumps it together with cranial osteopathy.
- **Biodynamic CST:**
  - Franklyn Sills's Karuna Institute (UK) went fully biodynamic from 1992.
  - The approach builds on Rollin Becker's "biodynamic potency": attending to a slow "Long Tide" rather than applying force.
- **In Poland:**
  - Courses for physiotherapists, masseurs, osteopaths and psychologists include Upledger-branded modules.
  - There are also two-year biodynamic schools, e.g. in Wrocław, accredited by a UK school.
  - I couldn't verify the course registry itself (the PARP site was down).

## 🔬 Is the mechanism plausible? 🟢 (against)

**Do skull bones move?** The textbook sceptic line is "sutures fuse in adolescence". That's too simple:

- Cadaver and CT studies show many sutures stay partly open into old age, and ossification varies by suture.
- So micro-motion is possible.
- The real problems are **how tiny it is** and whether **light touch can produce or feel it**:
  - In rabbits, 5–20 g "frontal lift" loads moved neither the suture nor intracranial pressure; separating the suture by 0.3 mm took about **500 g**.
  - A 1999 health-technology assessment found no study showing manual manipulation moves skull bones.

**Can therapists feel the rhythm? No.** Inter-rater reliability is essentially zero:

| Study | Setup | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Wirth-Pattullo & Hayes 1994 | 3 expert PTs, 12 subjects | inter-rater ICC **−0.02**; rates unrelated to heart or breathing rate |
| Rogers et al. 1998 | 28 subjects | inter-rater ICC 0.08 (head), 0.19 (feet); head and feet rates measured at the same time differed |
| Moran & Gibbons 2001 | 2 osteopaths, 11 subjects | inter-rater −0.09 to +0.31; head and sacrum rates differed, contradicting the claimed "core link" |
| Sommerfeld et al. 2004 | 49 subjects | agreement no better than chance; the **examiner's own breathing** influenced the rate felt |
| Guillaud et al. 2016 (systematic review) | 9 reliability studies, 8 high risk of bias | no reliability for any measure |

**What therapists may actually feel:**
- One candidate is **Traube-Hering-Mayer waves**, slow oscillations in blood pressure.
- The others are their own breathing and their own expectation.
- A 2024 systematic review of 28 articles calls THM waves the leading hypothesis and treats Sutherland's model as refuted.

## 🔬 Does it work? Systematic reviews

**Overall: no clinically meaningful benefit 🟢**

| Review | Scope | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Green et al. 1999 (BC HTA, *Complement Ther Med*) | 7 effectiveness studies | all at the lowest evidence grade; **insufficient evidence** |
| Jäkel & von Hauenschild 2011 (*JAOA*) | cranial osteopathy, 8 studies | evidence "heterogeneous and insufficient" |
| Jäkel & von Hauenschild 2012 (*Complement Ther Med*) | CST, 7 studies (3 RCTs) | research scarce, quality moderate at best |
| Ernst 2012 (*Focus Altern Complement Ther*) | 6 RCTs | 5 poor-quality, all positive; the one rigorous trial (cerebral palsy) **negative**; no effect beyond non-specific ones |
| Guillaud et al. 2016 (*PLoS One*) | 14 efficacy studies | **no evidence of specific efficacy** |
| **Haller et al. 2020** (*BMC Musculoskelet Disord*) | 10 RCTs, 681 pts, chronic pain | **favourable**: pain SMD −0.63 vs sham after treatment, −0.59 at 6 months (caveats below) |
| Carrasco-Uribarren et al. 2024 (*Pain Manag Nurs*) | headache, 4 RCTs | pain difference not clinically important; **very low certainty** |
| Ceballos-Laita et al. 2024 (*Healthcare*, Ernst co-author) | 15 RCTs, 7 pooled | no clinically relevant benefit for headache, neck, low back or pelvic girdle pain, or fibromyalgia; not effective for colic, preterm infants, cerebral palsy; **very low certainty** throughout |
| Amendolara et al. 2024 (*Front Med*, authors from an osteopathic college) | 24 RCTs, 1,613 participants | no significant effect on primary outcomes; CST lacks usefulness "for any of the studied indications" |

**Why the one positive review doesn't change the verdict 🟡/🔴.** Haller et al. 2020:
- pooled only **4 RCTs (N=230) against sham**, and only 2 (N=138) at 6 months;
- had patients blinded in only 2 trials, and therapists in none;
- gave no GRADE certainty rating;
- included the authors' own trial (Haller 2016, neck pain, 54 patients). That trial wasn't prospectively registered and never checked whether blinding worked.

The same group's later 220-patient cohort is telling: improvement was predicted by **patients' expectations and the therapist's experience**. Those are textbook non-specific effects.

**Children and infants 🟡/🔴.**
- **Infant colic.** Reviews of manual therapy in general (chiropractic and osteopathy, including cranial) show about 1.2 h/day less crying.
  - The effect **disappears when parents are blinded** (Cochrane, Dobson 2012).
  - A 2025 scoping review calls it at most "modest short-term relief".
- **Plagiocephaly.** Manual therapy in general beat repositioning, but nothing is CST-specific.
- **ADHD, autism, concussion.** There are only unblinded small trials, pilot studies and case reports, and no dedicated systematic review.

## ⚠️ Safety 🟡

- **As light touch: low risk.** Trials report no serious adverse events, though many trials don't report safety at all. Mild events in infants run about 7 per 1,000 treated.
- **Real harms, all outside gentle CST:**
  - 3 of 55 brain-injury patients (~5%) were harmed in an early case series.
  - A healthy 3-month-old died after forced deep flexion of the neck and spine by a "craniosacral therapist". A review of manual therapy in children counts 3 deaths, one of them under a craniosacral therapist.
  - **Avoid it for infants, after head injury, and anywhere force is used.**
- **Indirect harm:** delaying effective treatment because CST was sold as a cure.

## 🏛️ What regulators say

- **UK Advertising Standards Authority (2022 guidance):** no evidence that CST treats any condition. Advertisers may claim only relaxation and sensory effects. Colic claims were ruled misleading in 2011.
- **American Cancer Society (2009 guide; I saw it only via Wikipedia):** no evidence for treating cancer or any disease; it may ease stress; not recommended for children under 2.
- **Switzerland:** a registered complementary method, partly covered only by supplementary insurance. Unverified.
- **Poland (NFZ):** I found no position or reimbursement.

## 📒 My takeaways

1. **The diagnosis is not real.** Therapists can't agree on the rhythm, so "your cranial rhythm is blocked" carries no information.
2. **The treatment isn't specific.** Pain relief in trials is small, comes from small and poorly blinded studies, and shrinks toward zero in rigorous reviews.
3. **As a relaxing hour of gentle touch, it's harmless.** I'd treat it like a massage, never as a substitute for diagnosis or proven treatment. For desk-worker neck pain, active work beats passive touch; see [[Mobility for Desk Workers]] and [[Strength Training for Desk Workers 40+]].
4. **Red flags:** infants, head injury, forceful techniques, and claims about autism, ADHD, cancer or immunity.
