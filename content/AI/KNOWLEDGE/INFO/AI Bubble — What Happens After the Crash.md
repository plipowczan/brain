# AI Bubble — What Happens After the Crash

## 🚀 Main message

The video's thesis: an AI investment crash could destroy companies and investor capital while leaving useful technology and infrastructure behind. After an initial squeeze, cheaper infrastructure and inference could make everyday business automation economical. Technology adoption and returns to the investors financing it can follow different paths.

This is a summary of the author's scenario, not a prediction that a crash is certain or imminent. The financial figures and historical comparisons in the video have not been independently verified here.

## 🗒️ Description

- **Source:** Not Unprecedented, *The AI Bubble Will Burst. Here's What Happens Next*, published October 9, 2026; 13:03.
- **Framework:** the Gartner Hype Cycle, followed by three proposed phases: crash, clean-up, roll-out.
- **Useful distinction:** short-term AI service prices could rise as subsidies disappear, even if longer-term compute costs fall.

## 📒 Key points

1. **The hype cycle is a framework, not a calendar.** At [00:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=40s), the author places AI near the peak of inflated expectations. That placement is an interpretation rather than a demonstrated market-timing signal.
2. **Follow the financing chain.** At [01:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=113s), investors fund labs, labs rent cloud compute, and cloud operators buy chips. The author argues that end-customer revenue does not yet cover the whole chain's costs, leaving dependence on fresh capital and debt.
3. **Several events could break confidence.** At [03:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=214s), proposed triggers include a disappointing model release, a hyperscaler's spending cut, or a leveraged compute provider failing to refinance. The video also raises uncertainty about whether current LLM approaches lead to AGI.
4. **The first phase could hurt users as well as investors.** At [04:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=298s), the scenario spreads through concentrated equity markets, lenders, construction, equipment orders, and jobs. Providers losing investor subsidies could shrink free tiers and raise prices.
5. **A crash does not erase the installed infrastructure.** At [06:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=376s), the clean-up phase transfers data centers and chips to new owners at lower purchase prices. The author compares this with discounted fiber infrastructure after the dot-com boom. Electricity remains an operating cost even when capital costs are written down.
6. **Cheaper inference could unlock continuous business workflows.** At [07:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=463s), the example shifts from reading supplier confirmations to continuously checking orders against production plans and flagging delivery risks.
7. **The procurement example is illustrative.** At [08:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=522s), the author imagines annual checking costs falling from \$100,000 to \$2,000. This is a hypothetical 50-fold reduction, not a measured customer result or a price forecast. The manager's role shifts toward decisions and exceptions.
8. **Efficiency could increase total AI usage.** At [09:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=559s), the author invokes Jevons paradox: cheaper computation makes more applications worth running. This is the video's proposed demand response, not a guarantee for every workload.
9. **Abundant generation makes trust and judgment more valuable.** At [09:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=583s), cheaper content also means more disposable material. Provenance, accountability, and deciding what deserves to be made become differentiators; useful AI may disappear into ordinary software.
10. **Historical survival does not protect every investor.** At [10:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD0RcD9rq_c&t=655s), railway financing and the dot-com crash illustrate the separation between lasting infrastructure and failed investments. Analogies explain the proposed mechanism; they do not establish the timing or severity of an AI downturn.

## ☘️ What I take from this

- I would evaluate an AI workflow by the business task it improves and its total operating cost, rather than by the prominence of its model provider.
- The procurement example is a useful design prompt: ingest messy documents, compare them with operational data, detect exceptions, and give a person enough context to act.
- I would test whether a product still makes sense if free tiers disappear or inference prices temporarily rise. The video's cheaper long-term scenario should remain a scenario in that calculation.
- For consulting, the connection to [[AI 70-20-10 Rule]] is practical: integration, process redesign, and adoption determine whether cheaper intelligence produces useful work.
