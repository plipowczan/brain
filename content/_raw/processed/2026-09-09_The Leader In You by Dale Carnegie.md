#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Discover the leader in you.

Life’s in constant flux, but, barring an apocalypse, a few things will always remain the same – like the fact that you coexist with other people. And whether it’s in a professional or personal setting, the way you interact with them will make all the difference to your success and well-being. Research shows that even in technical fields like engineering, only 15 percent of financial success comes down to expertise; the rest – 85 percent – is due to personality and leadership skills. In other words, it pays to be smart... about your relationships with others. These blinks outline self-improvement guru Dale Carnegie’s timeless principles that have helped millions communicate and motivate effectively. With practice and a desire to learn, you, too, can find the leader in you – and revolutionize your life.

#### Chapter 1 of 13

## Identify your unique leadership strengths.

When you think about leaders, do presidents, generals, or movie directors come to mind? Leadership has an important role at the top – but it’s just as important at any level of this game called life. Want to energize your colleagues or keep things happy at home? Become a better leader. In fact, embodying leadership qualities will improve your experience immensely on all fronts. So what are these qualities? Well, there’s no one right way to lead. Leaders are sometimes loud and funny. Other times, they’re stoic, gentle, or even shy. The important thing is to figure out what kind of leader *you* are. Maybe you’re tenacious, creative, or always have a smile on your face; whatever your best qualities are, let them bloom. These are your foundation for all the leadership lessons to come. In Mother Teresa’s case, it was unabashed resolve. After visiting four inmates suffering from AIDS at a maximum-security prison, Mother Teresa marched to New York City Hall. It was the Monday before Christmas. In one sitting with the mayor, she secured medical furloughs so she could bring the inmates to the new AIDS center she was envisioning – as well as the necessary funding and permits for the building. The center opened that Wednesday. Failing was never an option.

#### Chapter 2 of 13

## Good communication is built on willingness, openness, and trust.

It used to be that loudness meant you were tough, stubbornness indicated knowledge, and being argumentative signified honesty. Here’s the thing: when it comes to getting people to do what you want, coercion and threats might work in the short term, but openness and support set the foundation for long-term success. Being able to communicate well can inspire people. It’s what catalyzes great ideas into action. And it’s not complicated if you master these three basic steps. First, make communication your top priority. We’re all busy – but that’s no excuse not to communicate. Remember, brilliant ideas are worthless if they’re not shared. Communication on the job can happen in many ways: during meetings, while walking down the hall, or in casual water-cooler moments. Second, be open to others. As with most things, communication is a two-way street. Showing your colleagues, clients, and friends that you’re receptive to their ideas will make them more likely to be receptive in turn. At home, try setting up weekly family meetings to discuss what’s on everyone’s minds. Third, create a hospitable environment for communication. People won’t say what they think, or truly listen, unless there’s a foundation of trust. That means being genuine – vulnerability is a great strength – and never discouraging others after they’ve shared their thoughts. After all, they’ve just taken a risk in opening up to you!

#### Chapter 3 of 13

## Motivating people through a common sense of purpose will make them perform better.

Picture a man on a horse, galloping toward the villain to rescue his one true love. As he rides, he’s joined by a guy on his left – then another on his right. Suddenly, there are ten more. They keep forging ahead, kicking up dust, multiplying in number. Seconds later, hundreds are riding toward their shared destiny. Like the hero in the cowboy movie trope, when a leader rides, others follow suit. To truly motivate people, you need to make them feel like you’re all in it together. Sure, paychecks and bonuses are great. But once people’s basic needs are met, real motivation never stems from financial incentives – or, for that matter, a fear of being fired. These methods just result in half-hearted efforts and resentment. Those united by common goals, on the other hand, won’t just do your bidding; they’ll go the extra mile because they *want* to. So give people what they truly crave: a sense of purpose. Do this by including them in the process. Ask for their advice, and follow it when you can; teamwork is key. Recognize people as people – smile, say hello, and let them know how valued and respected they are. Encourage and praise their efforts, celebrate your mutual triumphs, and commiserate shared failures. We all respond to expectations. Treat others as though they’re smart and capable – then get out of the way and watch your motivation tactics work their magic.

#### Chapter 4 of 13

## Express genuine interest in others to deepen your relationships.

Wagging tails, excited jumps, slobbery licks... dogs go out of their way to demonstrate they like you. And the best part is, you know their joy is genuine – ulterior motives aren’t part of their vocabulary. Dogs are the ultimate friend-makers. So take it from them: the fastest way of making friends is by becoming genuinely interested in others. People are flattered by attention; it makes them feel special. And so they tend to reciprocate. If you’re not used to expressing interest in others, it may feel forced at first. At work, try smiling and saying hi in a tone that implies you’re excited to see your colleagues. When you run into a familiar face on the street, remark on the happy coincidence. Learn names, as well as their spellings and pronunciations. Take note of birthdays, and ask about people’s families. Be sure to pay special attention to people who are often overlooked, like secretaries, and assistants. Ask how their days are going. It’s the decent thing to do – and you might just receive some perks in the process. Once you start expressing interest, it’ll naturally become a part of your life; you’ll find you’re genuinely more invested in those around you. As a result, your personal relationships will deepen and your negative thoughts will diminish. All that for a happy hello!

#### Chapter 5 of 13

## Looking at things from others’ perspectives is the key to getting along in the world.

David Holman had called his biggest grower with bad news: the export price for the man’s vegetables was going to be halved. The man sounded shocked, so Holman decided to drive to his farm and finish talking in person. Standing in the middle of a field in a spare pair of boots, he empathized with all the hard work, hardships, and disappointment the man had gone through. He expressed heartfelt concern. Miraculously, the grower accepted the situation – and Holman’s offer. This case is the perfect metaphor for the lesson here: looking at things from others’ perspectives is the best way to ease difficult situations and have more successful relationships. And thinking in terms of the interests of your boss, coworker, employee, customer, spouse, friend, or child will pave the way for an open, productive dialogue. Don’t just wait around for suggestions or complaints to roll in. Instead, proactively ask questions: What life experiences does the other person bring to the table? What is she trying to achieve or avoid? What unspoken issues are at stake? You don’t need to blindly go along with everything. Just make a sincere effort to figure out what other people desire, and do your best to solve their problems. If you can do that, the world’s your oyster.

#### Chapter 6 of 13

## Listening is the best form of learning – and of persuasion.

Back when Hugh Downs was just getting started as a live radio interviewer, he witnessed a grandiose failure to listen. A colleague was interviewing a man who’d escaped a Kremlin prison. It was a dramatic tale. For months, the man said, the prisoners had been tunneling their way out – sometimes even eating dirt. Finally, they began digging upward and were shocked when they breached the surface: they’d landed smack-dab in the middle of Josef Stalin’s office. At that point, the interviewer asked whether the man had any hobbies. Major fail. Good listening is where effective communication begins – it’s more important than having a way with words, a stirring voice, or the ability to speak many languages. There are two main reasons for this. One, you’ll learn things. No one can know everything, but you can access a wealth of data by tapping into others’ brains. And two, people respond to those who listen to them. We all love feeling understood and valued. Because of this, listening to, internalizing, and mirroring another’s opinions can actually persuade that person to see the world the way you do. True listening isn’t a passive act; it requires total focus. To show you’re engaged, try nodding occasionally, saying uh-huh, or shifting posture. Strong eye contact is key. And when the other person pauses, ask a thoughtful, targeted question.

#### Chapter 7 of 13

## Effective teamwork results in creativity, innovation, and long-term success.

One year, instead of getting a midterm exam, first-year graduate students at Harvard Business School were randomly divided into teams of four. They were given a business problem and 24 hours to draft a written solution. Oh, and each team member would receive the same grade. Needless to say, the students weren’t happy. They thought their team members would bring them down. But, in the end, the groups that struggled the most learned the most – welcome to the real world. Big organizations used to be pyramid-shaped. The base comprised workers, topped by layers of managers. The CEO sat at the pyramid’s pinnacle, like an eagle in its nest. But, as it turns out, rigid hierarchies, departmental lines, and complex chains of command stifle creative work. Organizations fare a lot better if the boss-subordinate structure is replaced by teams. When hierarchies are broken down, people are free to reach their full potential and engage in constructive conflict. Great teamwork takes cooperative players and a savvy coach. You’ve already learned how important it is to create a shared sense of purpose, recognize people’s individual talents, and share the glory – as well as the blame. A good leader also builds confidence and shows that she believes in the team. She stays involved. And she acts as a mentor, becoming invested in the lives and careers of the team members. If your team succeeds, you succeed. And you’ll have left a great legacy by creating a group of talented, motivated, and cooperative people: the leaders of tomorrow.

#### Chapter 8 of 13

## Be quick to admit your own mistakes and slow to criticize others – and always be constructive.

Nobody’s perfect; everyone makes mistakes. We all know this on some level – but it’s only once mistakes have been fully normalized that people can truly express their creativity or take risks. There are a few basic principles that can make criticism an easier pill to swallow. For starters, be the first to admit your own mistakes. Maybe even laugh about them. And never try to minimize their impact. In a funny twist of human psychology, this won’t just help normalize mistakes; it’ll also soften the mood when blame’s being laid. People will reassure you that things aren’t that bad after all. This is true for any relationship, be it with a colleague, family member, friend, or customer. Conversely, if you blame others, they’ll immediately begin contradicting you and defending their actions. People don’t usually try to mess up. If someone does stumble, he probably already knows how and why it happened – and what needs to be done to prevent future blunders. So think twice before pointing the finger at someone. When criticism *is* warranted – maybe the issue at hand is too urgent or happens too frequently to let slide – remember to be constructive, always. Follow Mary Poppins’s lead and deliver your medicine with a spoonful of sugar: maybe some praise at what’s being done well. Or let the person save face by asking questions instead of giving orders. It’s all about finesse. If you’re arguing with someone, if you’re using words like wrong and stupid, you’ve already lost. Because the goal isn’t really to prove you’re right, is it? It’s to communicate, motivate, and persuade.

#### Chapter 9 of 13

## Setting goals that are clear, measurable, and realistic will give your life direction.

Endless drifting. Wasted time. Zero urgency, zero direction, zero purpose. No, we’re not talking about purgatory. We’re talking about a life devoid of goals. Clear, measurable, and attainable goals can give your life direction and keep you focused. Legendary tennis player Arthur Ashe set career goals until the day he died. As soon as he achieved one, he’d set another. And he found that the self-confidence he got from reaching his goals spilled into every other area of his life. So don’t settle for the status quo. Always embrace the opportunity to try something new, or a way of improving upon what you’re already doing. Periodically ask yourself who you really want to be and what kind of life you truly want to lead. Then reflect on whether you’re currently headed in that direction. The answers to these questions might change and develop over the years, and that’s fine! It’s important to set both short- and long-term goals – after all, Rome wasn’t built in a day. That’s where interim goals come into play. Setting little goals and achieving them will boost your confidence and motivate you to tackle new, bigger projects. Sometimes you’ll meet your deadlines, sometimes things will take longer than expected, and sometimes you won’t succeed at all. All that matters is that you keep planning, evolving, and persevering. Step by step, you’ll find yourself accomplishing extraordinary things. As business author Harvey Mackay said, a goal is just a dream with a deadline.

#### Chapter 10 of 13

## To succeed, stay focused and keep your eye on the big picture.

David Burpee thought the marigold had potential. Back in 1933, the flower was typically small – and it smelled bad. So Burpee set out to find a mutation without the unpleasant odor. He ordered hundreds of seeds from all over the world, planting and then sniffing every flower. One variety from Tibet was promising: it was odorless but had a scrawny bloom. Encouraged, Burpee crossed the Tibetan seed with a larger variety and planted 35 acres. Once the flowers had bloomed, he told his foreman to go and smell every plant. The man looked at him like he’d gone bonkers, so they came up with a compromise. Burpee called up all the employment agencies in the area with an unusual request: 200 flower smellers. They soon got to work. And, at last, Burpee’s singular vision paid off. One of the plants yielded a large, neutral-smelling flower – which, today, graces countless gardens across the US. The point is, success isn’t that complicated. What it does require is laser-sharp focus, self-discipline, and a desire to be better than you are right now. Once you’ve articulated your goals, you need to believe in yourself, be persistent, and banish distractions. But what happens when something goes wrong? Well, you stand up, brush yourself off, and keep going. As you know, mistakes and failure are totally normal. It’s important to remember that – especially in the face of discouragement, failure, or despair. So, as the saying goes, try, try again... and again and again!

#### Chapter 11 of 13

## Maximize your performance by balancing work and play.

Here’s a riddle for you: What do the US Army, your heart, and Winston Churchill have in common? The answer is an oxymoron: they all figured out that pressing pause is the best way to keep going. The US Army discovered that soldiers could march further and faster if they laid down their packs and rested for 10 minutes of every hour. So that’s what the soldiers do. Your heart pumps a lot of blood through your body each day – enough to fill a railway tank car. But it takes a break after each contraction. On average, it only really works for 9 out of the 24 hours. And Winston Churchill was powering through 16-hour days well into his sixties as he dealt with World War II. What was his secret? Taking naps and doing work from bed – and the bathtub. Instead of dealing with burnout after the fact, he prevented it altogether by resting frequently. Ambitious people typically put their careers first; work can just seem so much more urgent and important. But what’s truly important is balance. Making time for other things – like family, personal passions, or exercise – will yield happier, healthier personal lives. As an added bonus, it’ll also leave you more energized, focused, and productive at work. To find balance, first you need to change your mindset: stop thinking of relaxation as wasted time. Then, schedule in time for leisure. This might mean reevaluating priorities and cutting back on commitments. Try devoting as much time to planning fun as you do to planning your workday. Now it’s time to act. Do something – anything – that’s not work-related. Take a walk with your family every evening. Set up alone time to meditate, be creative, or read. Even chores like washing the dishes or raking leaves can be therapeutic.

#### Chapter 12 of 13

## Reenergize your life by looking on the bright side and taming your worries.

Think happy thoughts, and you’ll be happy. Think miserable thoughts, and you’ll be miserable. Think successful thoughts, and – well, you get it. Outside factors don’t usually determine your happiness; it’s how you react that makes all the difference. That’s right: simply assuming a positive attitude can work wonders in terms of your productivity, quality of life, and ability to lead. So how do you develop a positive outlook? For one, continually remind yourself that your thoughts have the power to shape your reality. Try surrounding yourself with happy, successful people; their positive energy will rub off on you. A few useful tactics can help dissipate worries. Try picturing the worst-case scenario, and talk yourself through how you’d manage it. What steps could you take to improve the situation? Who else might be able to help? Accept the inevitable. Sometimes you’ll be skilled or lucky enough to solve challenging circumstances. But there’ll always be other things you can’t fix. Instead of getting disappointed or bitter about them, focus on what you *can* control. This ties into perspective. Some things in life really matter; others don’t. Learning to tell them apart can be a boon to your happiness levels. Is your worry worth a sleepless night? A week of anxiety? An ulcer? Don’t sweat the small stuff. And if you’re stuck in an emotional rut, distract your mind by getting busy: help others, dive into a project, or learn a new skill.

#### Chapter 13 of 13

Some people are blessed with smarts. Others have a penchant for creativity or athletics. But, luckily, raw talent is only half the picture. Each and every one of us can add lines, flourishes, and color to our pictures with a little thing called enthusiasm. Enthusiasm is infectious, and it engages people. The best way to get someone excited about something is to be excited yourself – and to show it. Showing your enthusiasm doesn’t mean being loud. In fact, true enthusiasm, a mixture of eagerness and assurance, is often quiet. Back in the 1950s, Jonas Salk was developing the polio vaccine. His pure enthusiasm was evident in his intense lab sessions and in the way his eyes sparkled when he talked about his research. You can’t fake enthusiasm – but you can plant it, nourish it, and make it work for you. Start by telling yourself what you like about your life or vocation, and why. Then share these thoughts with someone else. If you *act* as though you’re interested in, say, your job, you might eventually find you really *are*. It’s easy to tap into your enthusiasm when you have things to look forward to – another reason goals are so important. When you wake up each morning, take a moment to reflect on some positive experience you’ll have that day. It could be something seemingly unremarkable: an aspect of your job, a rendezvous with friends, or a dance class. And if you truly can’t find anything to look forward to at work or in your personal life, do something about it!

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

**Becoming a better leader entails becoming a better person. Build off your inherent strengths, and hone your communication skills – that includes genuinely listening, putting yourself in other people’s shoes, and voicing your appreciation. Empower your peers by making room for mistakes, being enthusiastic, and always acting with respect. And, for your own peace of mind, be sure to keep your priorities clear and to balance work with play. Once you put these behaviors into practice, you’ll find yourself succeeding not only at the office, but in every aspect of life.**
