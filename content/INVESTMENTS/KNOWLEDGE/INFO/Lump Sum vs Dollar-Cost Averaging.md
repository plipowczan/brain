# Lump Sum vs Dollar-Cost Averaging

## 🗒️ Description

The question: I have a pile of cash (savings, a bonus, the proceeds of a sale) and a target allocation. Do I invest it all now (**lump sum**) or in instalments over a few months (**dollar-cost averaging**, DCA, also called cost averaging)? This is different from investing a salary every month, where there is no pile to stage.

This note collects the evidence and the arguments on both sides. It is knowledge and method, not advice. My own re-entry plan, without amounts, is in [[My Investment Strategy]].

**TL;DR**

- **Lump sum wins about two times out of three.** Vanguard (2012, 2023) found 66–68% across the US, UK, Australia, Canada, Europe and global portfolios; practitioner studies range from 56% to 92%.
- **The average gap is small, the losing cases are not.** Over a year the median gap is about 2% for an all-equity portfolio; when lump sum loses, it can lose by 20–30% of the sum after a crash.
- **DCA is a temporary lower allocation.** Theory and simulations agree that holding a smaller equity share from day one does the same job as staging, more cheaply.
- **Waiting for a dip is worse than both** in every study found.
- **All-time highs are not a reason to wait.** Starting at a high, lump sum won 91% of 12-month windows. Only very expensive stocks relative to bonds brought DCA close to even.
- **If I stage, keep it short and fixed:** about three to six months, on dates set in advance.
- **For a PLN-based investor** no direct study exists. The main local differences are the after-tax yield on waiting cash, FX fees per tranche and the yearly limits on tax wrappers.

## 🧩 The Vanguard Studies

| | Vanguard 2012 (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos, Wimmer) | Vanguard 2023 (Finlay, Zorn) |
|---|---|---|
| Data | US 1926–2011, UK 1976–2011, Australia 1984–2011 | MSCI World 1976–2022 and nine markets |
| DCA window | 12 months (also 36) | 3 months (also 4–6) |
| Judged over | 10 years | 1 year |
| Lump sum won | 67% US, 67% UK, 66% Australia (60/40) | 68% (100% equity) |
| Gap | 2.3% US, 2.2% UK, 1.3% Australia (ending value) | Median 2.2% (100% equity), 1.8% (60/40) |
| Cash assumption | Local T-bills | 0% base case; with T-bill interest lump sum still won 65% |

- **Every market agreed** (Vanguard 2023, lump sum vs three-month averaging): US 66.4%, UK 68.1%, Canada 67.2%, Europe in EUR 66.5%, Australia 67.5%, emerging markets 61.6%, global in EUR 66.4%.
- **Longer averaging is worse.** Against 36-month DCA, lump sum won about 90% of the time in the US (2012). In the 2023 simulation, three-month averaging ended 504 below lump sum per 100,000 invested, six-month 1,491 below.
- **The downside tail is real.** In the 2012 paper, at the 5th percentile lump sum trailed DCA by 20–30% of the starting sum after 10 years. Over 1,021 rolling 12-month US periods, lump sum lost money 22.4% of the time against 17.6% for DCA.
- **Vanguard's own advice** (2023): keep any averaging short, about three months, and pay a year's full tax-wrapper contribution at the start of the year. The 2012 paper adds that an investor uneasy about a lump sum should revisit the target allocation instead.

## 🧩 Practitioner Backtests

| Study | Setup | Lump sum won |
|---|---|---|
| Morningstar, Kowara and Kaplan (2019) | US; DCA over 10 months and 10 years | 72% and 90% (DCA won 27.8% and 10.0%) |
| Northwestern Mutual (2021) | Rolling 10-year windows from 1950 | 75% equities, 80% 60/40, 90% bonds |
| Early Retirement Now (2017) | US 1871–2017, 12-month DCA | 56–62%; mean 12-month excess return 6.29% vs 3.33% |
| PWL Capital, Felix (2020) | Six markets in USD | 64.9% on average, ahead by 0.38% a year over 10 years |
| Morgan Stanley GIO (Apr 2025) | Over 1,000 seven-year periods | More than 56% |
| AAII, Fisher (Mar 2026) | 958 rolling 20-year periods | 72.65%, ahead by 0.22% a year |

None of these models FX conversion, per-tranche fees or tax on the interest earned by waiting cash; all are USD index returns.

## 🧩 What Theory Says

- **DCA ignores information.** Constantinides (JFQA 1979): a fixed instalment plan cannot be optimal, because a strategy that reacts to new information dominates it.
- **Invest less, not later.** Rozeff (JPM 1994): lump sum beat 12-month DCA in 40 of 65 years of the S&P 500 (1926–1990). An investor who wants less risk should put a smaller fraction in now, all at once.
- **The "lower average cost" argument is a mistake.** Hayley (2012, 2015): comparing DCA's average cost with the average price implicitly compares it with impossible perfect foresight. DCA is inefficient whatever the investor's preferences over final wealth; for 12 monthly tranches the loss is 0.12–0.48% of capital.
- **The exposure confound.** Cho and Kuvvet (JFP 2015) and Kirkby, Mitra and Nguyen (EJOR 2020) show DCA with lower risk and sometimes better utility, but mostly because DCA holds less equity on average. A one-year DCA into two tranches is simply a 50/50 mix for that year. My research run's check on Cho and Kuvvet's numbers: a static 74.5% equity allocation matches DCA's mean return with lower volatility.
- **Momentum can help DCA a little.** O'Brien and Piros (2022 working paper): with short-term momentum followed by reversion, an optimal DCA beat the optimal lump sum by 10–54 bp over three years; under a random walk, lump sum won by 40–215 bp.
- **Valuation as a switch.** Luskin (JFP 2017) found 15-year DCA won every period that started with CAPE above 31, but those periods are essentially one bubble, and his own follow-up warns the result may not apply today.

## 🧩 Entering at All-Time Highs or High Valuations

- **Highs are normal, not a warning.** Starting at a month-end all-time high, lump sum won 91.1% of 12-month windows, against about 65% otherwise (AAII, Fisher 2026). J.P. Morgan data for 1988–2024, as tabulated by Motley Fool, show forward returns after highs equal to or slightly above those from an average day.
- **Expensive on CAPE alone is not enough.** Bernstein (Ding Liu, 2025, US 1926–2024): in the most expensive CAPE quintile DCA still trailed by a median 3.1%.
- **Expensive relative to bonds comes closest.** In the lowest excess-CAPE-yield quintile (equity earnings yield minus real bond yield), DCA won 48% of the time, the median shortfall fell to 0.6 points, and the bad tail improved (10th percentile −17.6% instead of −31.6%). Bernstein put that yield at 1.9% at the end of Q1 2025, the lowest since June 2007. The quintiles use the whole sample, which builds in look-ahead bias.
- **Carlson (2018):** at CAPE of 32 or more, lump sum still won 60%, but in losing years it lost about 15.5% against about 8% for DCA. That is two bubble episodes and 50 overlapping windows.
- **Bernstein's rule:** if staging, average in over six months or less, never beyond 18 months. Buying only on dips made results worse.

## 🧩 Waiting for a Dip

- **Elm Partners** (Haghani and White, 7 Aug 2017), expensive starting markets: a 10% fall below the starting price came within three years only 56% of the time. Waiting gained about 10% when it did and cost about 30% when it did not, an average cost of about 8%. A plan to buy only after a 10% fall never invested in about one case in three over ten years.
- **Maggiulli (2019, 2021):** plain monthly investing beat a buyer with perfect knowledge of every bottom in over 70% of rolling 40-year windows. Buying only after 10–20% falls lost to monthly investing about 75% of the time.
- **Coutts** (Aylott, 5 Jan 2026), S&P 500 1990–2024: investing on the first day of each year averaged 12.1% a year; waiting for a 10% correction averaged 6.6%. Lombard Odier (Nov 2025) found waiting cost 5.4% a year even with the cash earning T-bill interest.

## 🧩 Volatile Assets: Emerging Markets, Small Caps, Bitcoin

- **Emerging markets** gave lump sum its lowest win rate in Vanguard 2023 (61.6–62.9% in USD, 1988–2022).
- **Bernstein (2025), 12-month DCA, 1988–2024:** DCA won 41% in emerging markets, 36% in US small caps and 24% in the S&P 500, with the largest benefit in poor markets (+15.2% in emerging markets).
- **The driver is return relative to volatility, not volatility alone.** Emerging markets and small caps lagged over that period. Bitcoin's very high returns kept lump sum ahead despite its volatility.
- **Bitcoin evidence is vendor-only.** Bull Bitcoin (2016 – May 2025): DCA won about 17.5% of the time. MarketVector, entries at least 50% below the all-time high: one-year median +99% for lump sum vs +50% for DCA, worst case −63% vs −50%. With small tranches, fixed fees can eat most of DCA's benefit.

## 🧩 The Behavioural Case and Hybrid Entries

- **DCA is "not rational but normal".** Statman (1995, 2018) explains it by framing, regret and self-control, and calls it wise for regret-prone investors who would otherwise not invest at all.
- **The utility case is thin.** In Vanguard's 2023 model, three-month averaging was preferred only by a very conservative investor (risk aversion 10), or a moderately conservative one once loss aversion of 2.5 was added. The winning margins were 0.02–0.33 percentage points.
- **Better alternatives for the nervous investor:** invest the whole sum now in a more conservative mix (Vanguard 2012; Felix; Maggiulli), or a hybrid. Elm estimates putting about half in now and the rest over a year captures about 75% of the benefit of going all in.
- **Pre-commitment matters.** Fixed dates and automatic transfers stop the plan from stalling. Several sources warn that people pause DCA exactly when prices fall, when it would help; no study measures how often that happens.

## 🧩 A PLN-Based Investor

- **No direct study exists.** I found no lump-sum vs DCA backtest for global equities measured in PLN. The closest is Vanguard's EUR series (MSCI World in EUR, 1998–2022): lump sum won 66.4% against a three-month split.
- **Currency timing is a separate decision.** Oliveira (CFA Institute, 5 Nov 2026), for a Brazilian investor buying the S&P 500 in 2000–2026: converting at once every month beat a timer with perfect foresight of exchange rates in 80% of ten-year windows. Set the allocation first and treat currency separately.
- **Waiting cash earns less after tax.** Interest on deposits, savings accounts and bonds is taxed at 19% outside wrappers: the NBP reference rate of 3.75% (since 5 Mar 2026) becomes about 3.0% net. From 2027, OKI exempts savings assets within its limit; see [[Polish Investor Taxes 2026]].
- **FX fees.** A percentage conversion fee costs the same whether I convert once or six times; only fixed or minimum fees grow with the number of tranches. Secondary sources say some brokers' IKE/IKZE accounts are PLN-only, so the conversion fee applies to every foreign ETF purchase there.
- **Wrappers force staging anyway.** IKE, IKZE and OIPE have yearly limits, so a large sum cannot go in at once. Because the yearly limit resets on 1 January, a contribution at the end of December and another in early January land a few weeks apart.

## ☘️ What I Take From It

- **Default to investing at once**, at an allocation I can hold through a crash. If the lump sum feels too risky, the honest fix is a lower equity weight, not a delay.
- **If I stage, three to six months on fixed dates,** written down before I start, with no "wait for a better price" clause.
- **All-time highs and scary headlines are not reasons to wait;** only extreme valuations relative to bonds have any support, and even then it is a coin flip.
- **For crypto** the evidence is vendor-only; staging there is about managing my own behaviour and the sleeve cap in [[My Crypto Strategy]], not about expected return.
- **Fill the tax wrappers first** each year, as early in the year as possible.
